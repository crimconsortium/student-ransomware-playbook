Protect yourself — citation view

A handful of habits that make your accounts, devices, and coursework a lot harder to ransom or steal. [guidance] You don't need to be technical to do any of this. [guidance]

Plain view →

This page paraphrases public guidance from CISA Secure Our World, the National Cybersecurity Alliance, and EDUCAUSE. [guidance] It's general advice for advance reading. [1] If something's happening right now, call campus IT from a phone or another device you trust. [guidance]

Do these before anything goes wrong

  • Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) for your campus account, email, and any system holding your records. [authority: CISA]
  • Use a password manager and a unique password for your campus identity. [guidance] Don't reuse it on other sites. [guidance]
  • Keep your laptop, phone, and browser updated. [guidance] Install updates within 7 days of release. [guidance]
  • Back up your coursework to campus cloud storage (and a second copy where allowed). [guidance] Don't rely on a single drive. [guidance]
  • Save the IT help desk and security team contact info in your phone before you need it. [guidance]
  • Complete any phishing or security training the campus offers. [guidance] Even short modules build real skill. [guidance]
  • Review your account recovery options (backup email, phone) so an attacker can't reset your password. [guidance]

Phishing red flags

Most attacks on students start with a phish. [2] An email, text, DM, or fake login page that looks real. [guidance] Slow down if you see any of this: [guidance]

  • "Your account will be locked in 24 hours. [guidance] "Your aid will be canceled." [guidance]
  • A login link in the message. [guidance] Don't click it. [guidance] Type your campus URL into the browser yourself. [guidance]
  • A sender domain that's close but not exact (like support@my-college.edu.help instead of support@my-college.edu). [guidance]
  • Unexpected attachments. [guidance] Especially zip files, OneNote files, or HTML "invoices." [guidance]
  • An offer that's too good to be true. [guidance] High-paying remote job for a student, scholarship you never applied for, free laptop, surprise refund. [guidance]
  • MFA prompts you didn't trigger. [guidance] Never approve a prompt you didn't start. [guidance] Repeated prompts are MFA fatigue. [guidance] It's a known attack. [guidance]

And sometimes there are no red flags.

A real account from someone you actually know — a friend, a professor, your RA, the campus help desk — can be compromised and used to send phishing. [3] Same name, same email address, normal-looking signature. [guidance] The message will look legitimate because, technically, it is. [guidance] If a message asks you to click a link, log in somewhere, pay something, share an MFA code, or move money, treat the request with suspicion — not just the sender. [guidance] Verify out-of-band: text or call the person on a number you already had, or walk over. [guidance]
