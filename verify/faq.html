FAQ — citation view

Short answers to the questions students actually ask about ransomware, phishing, and account safety. [guidance]

Plain view →

What even is ransomware, in plain terms?

Software that locks up your files (and usually steals copies first) so someone can charge you to unlock them, or to not leak them. [guidance] As a student you mostly see the way in: phishing emails, fake login pages, and account takeovers. [1]

Why should I care? Isn't this an IT problem?

Your account is the easiest way in. [guidance] Universities have documented student accounts being taken over by phishing, then used to send more phishing to classmates from a trusted address, and to reset passwords or change account-recovery settings so attackers keep access even after the original password is changed. [2] A single careless click can affect your grades, financial aid, and the people around you. [guidance]

I think I clicked a phishing link. What now?

Stop using the device. [guidance] From a different device (your phone is fine), change the password for the affected account, then call the campus IT help desk. [guidance] Keep the original message. [guidance] IT may need it. [guidance] Speed matters more than embarrassment. [guidance]

My laptop is showing a ransom note. What should I do?

Don't pay anything. [authority: CISA] Disconnect from Wi-Fi and unplug any cables, but leave the device powered on (forensics may need volatile memory). [3] Call campus IT right now. [guidance] Don't try to 'fix' it yourself. [guidance]

Is the campus going to discipline me if I report a mistake?

Many campuses encourage honest reporting and treat accidental clicks differently from intentional misconduct. [guidance] They need the data to defend everyone. [guidance] Check your campus policy, but in general the cost of not reporting is worse than the cost of reporting. [guidance]

Should I use a personal device for schoolwork?

Generally fine if it's up to date, has MFA on your campus account, and you don't pirate software on it. [guidance] Some courses or programs (e.g., clinical, legal, research with sensitive data) require institution-managed devices. [guidance] Check your syllabus. [guidance]

Is MFA actually worth the hassle?

Yes. [4] Multi-factor authentication is one of the most important things you can do to protect an account, and CISA recommends it for everyone. [5] Phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2 / passkeys / hardware keys like YubiKey) is the strongest form because the credential is bound to the legitimate site. [guidance] Enable it wherever your campus offers it. [guidance]

Is public Wi-Fi at the coffee shop dangerous?

Less than it used to be. [guidance] Most sites and apps use HTTPS by default. [guidance] Still a good idea to avoid logging into your campus account from networks you don't trust, and to use your campus VPN if your school provides one. [guidance]

Can this site substitute for legal advice?

No. [guidance] This is a plain-language educational summary. [6] Specific obligations under FERPA, state breach laws, or your campus policies depend on your situation. [guidance] Talk to your IT help desk, dean of students, or campus counsel. [guidance]
